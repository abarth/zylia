# Story Principles

What makes a role-playing game story compelling, and the rules every story
draft for Zylia is held to. The review rubric in [review.md](review.md)
turns these into questions.

These are drawn from what makes Final Fantasy IV and VI endure: a cast you
care about, a villain you fear, real losses, and a world that changes because
of what happens in it.

## 1. A question worth two dozen hours

- The story has a **dramatic question** the player wants answered (can the
  party stop X before Y?) and a **theme**: a question about how to live that
  the story argues about rather than states (FF6: what do you live for when
  the world has ended?).
- The theme is an argument, not a moral. **Each major character embodies a
  different answer** to the thematic question, including the antagonist.
  Their conflicts are the argument.

## 2. Characters drive the story

- Every playable character has a concrete **want** (what they pursue), a
  **need** (what they must learn or accept), and a **wound** (why they
  don't have it yet). Their arc is change under pressure, and it costs them.
- The party is an **ensemble**. Each character's arc puts pressure on at least
  one other character's arc. Relationships change over the story.
- Characters make **choices** that cause events. A character who only reacts
  or only explains is not yet finished.
- Each character is **distinct at a glance**: silhouette, voice, battle role
  and personal plot all point the same way.

## 3. An antagonist who acts

- The antagonist has a goal the player can understand, and in some light
  sympathize with. Their plan **causes** the plot even when they are off
  screen: the party is reacting to their moves.
- The antagonist **escalates**: they get closer to their goal, and at least
  once they win.
- The antagonist is a **dark mirror**: they answer the thematic question in a
  way that tests the protagonists' answer.
- Menace is shown, not told: the player sees the antagonist's power and
  cruelty (or conviction) in scenes, early.

## 4. Stakes that rise and stay personal

- Stakes escalate from personal to communal to world, but world-scale stakes
  stay tied to specific people and places the player has come to care about.
- Victories cost something. Losses are real and stay lost; if the game
  allows revival in battle, the story must explain why a story death is
  different, or avoid the contradiction.

## 5. Cause and effect

- Beats connect with **"therefore"** and **"but"**, never **"and then"**.
  Every chapter ends in a way that causes the next.
- Coincidence may get the party into trouble, never out of it.

## 6. Promises and payoffs

- Every major reveal is **set up at least twice** before it lands. Every
  setup is **paid off**. Both are tracked in [canon/threads.md](canon/threads.md).
- Twists **recontextualize** what came before; on a replay, the clues were
  there. A twist that makes the player's earlier effort meaningless is not
  allowed.
- Mysteries are introduced at a steady rate and answered at a steady rate;
  the player always has open questions, and regularly gets answers.

## 7. Story and play reinforce each other

- A character's abilities express who they are. Party changes (joining,
  leaving, dying) happen at story beats and change how the game plays.
- Story beats happen in places the player explores; dungeons have a reason
  to exist in the world and in the plot.
- Open-world freedom is designed for: beats that may happen in any order are
  written to work in any order, and the story acknowledges what the player
  chose to do.
- The player is never asked to do something the characters wouldn't, or
  prevented from something they obviously would, without a reason in the
  story.

## 8. Pacing and texture

- Alternate tension and release. Towns, camps and quiet scenes are where
  relationships deepen. Humor is part of the texture, including in dark
  stretches.
- Each chapter has at least one **set piece**: a memorable, specific scene
  the player will retell (FF6's opera, FF4's Mysidian twins).
- Exposition comes in small doses, from characters with a reason to say it,
  preferably while something else is happening.

## 9. Specific, not generic

- Concrete, surprising, specific details beat generic fantasy. A town is
  known for one thing; a culture has one custom nobody else has.
- Familiar tropes (the chosen one, amnesia, the evil empire, the crystals)
  are used only on purpose, with a twist that makes them ours. The review
  flags unexamined tropes.
- Nothing is copied from Final Fantasy or other works: names, places,
  characters and plots are original. We borrow structures and feelings, not
  content.

## 10. An ending that answers

- The ending answers the dramatic question, completes the thematic argument,
  and pays off every playable character's arc, including what happens to
  them after.
- The final confrontation is with the antagonist's ideas as much as their
  power.
